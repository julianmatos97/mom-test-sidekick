# The Mom Test — research digest for the coach prompt

Compiled 2026-07-03 from a multi-source research pass (primary sources: the book text,
Fitzpatrick's own summary deck, and a Sept 2025 author Q&A; cross-checked against ~15
practitioner summaries). Feeds `SYSTEM_PROMPT` in `src/momtest/coach.py`.

## The three rules (verbatim, p. 13)
1. Talk about their life instead of your idea.
2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.
3. Talk less and listen more.

"If you just avoid mentioning your idea, you automatically start asking better questions."
Named because it produces questions even your mom can't lie to you about.

## Three types of bad data + recovery (pp. 23–36)
| Type | Signals | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Compliments | "sounds great", "I love it" — "the fool's gold of customer learning" | Deflect, return to their life. Compliments usually mean you were pitching. |
| Fluff | generics ("I usually/always/never"), future promises ("I would/I will"), hypotheticals ("I might/I could") | Anchor to the past: "When did that last happen? Talk me through it." |
| Ideas | feature requests | Dig beneath: "Why do you want that? What would it let you do? How are you coping without it?" |

- "The world's most deadly fluff is: 'I would definitely buy that.'" (a startup Fitzpatrick
  worked at lost ~$10M to that trap). Worst fluff-inducing question: "Would you ever…?"
- Pitch-slip recovery script: "Oops — I just slipped into pitch mode. I'm really sorry —
  I get excited about these things. Can we jump back to what you were saying?"

## Question bank (book-rated, pp. 14–20)
Good: "Talk me through the last time that happened." · "What else have you tried?" ·
"How are you dealing with it now?" (also a price anchor) · "Why do you bother?" ·
"What are the implications of that?" (pay-to-solve vs live-with-it) · "Where does the
money come from?" · "Who else should I talk to?" (end every conversation with this) ·
"Is there anything else I should have asked?" · Emotion-digging: "Tell me more about
that." / "That seems to really bug you — I bet there's a story here." / "What makes it
so awful?" / "Why haven't you been able to fix this already?"

Bad/awful: "Do you think it's a good idea?" · "Would you buy a product which did X?" ·
"How much would you pay for X?"

Rule of thumb: if they haven't already looked for ways to solve it, they're not going to
look for (or buy) yours.

## Commitment & advancement (pp. 62–68)
- "There's no such thing as a meeting which just 'went well'. Every meeting either
  succeeds or fails." Success = ends with a commitment to advance.
- Currencies: **time** (clear next meeting with known goals; non-trivial trial),
  **reputation risk** (intro to peers/team/boss/decision-maker; public testimonial),
  **money** (LOI, pre-order, deposit). "The more they're giving up, the more seriously
  you can take their kind words." "People stop lying when you ask them for money."
- Failure modes: compliments without commitment; "keep me posted" / stalling; **zombie
  leads** (stay friendly, never advance). Causes: fishing for compliments; not asking
  for a clear commitment.
- Author Q&A (2025): early on, keep learning and selling separate — ask for time or
  reputation commitments, not money. Conditional-time ask: "You've given me a lot to
  think about. Can I come back in ~3 weeks when X is ready and check it would solve
  this?" — the more concrete the trigger, the more the yes is worth. To pitch after a
  learning call, ask permission and respect a no.

## Segmentation & call hygiene
- Customer slicing: narrow the segment into who-where pairs until problems and goals are
  consistent; inconsistent findings = segment too broad. "Before we can serve everyone,
  we have to serve someone."
- Meeting framing (cold outreach): Vision → Framing → Weakness → Pedestal → Ask.
- Common founder mistakes: pitching instead of listening ("The more you're talking, the
  worse you're doing"), fishing for approval, premature zoom into a problem before
  confirming it matters, treating politeness as validation, one founder hoarding all
  customer learning, endless interviewing without building, no scary questions ("You
  should be terrified of at least one of the questions you're asking in every
  conversation").
